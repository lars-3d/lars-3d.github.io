Linear Global-Context Aggregation in Sparse Convolutional Networks for Robust Outdoor 3D Point Cloud Segmentation

Abstract

Urban-trained models break down on terrain

Recent advances in LiDAR semantic segmentation have largely focused on structured urban driving datasets, where sparse convolutional and attention-based models achieve strong performance. However, their behavior in unstructured, terrain-rich environments remains underexplored. In this work, we investigate semantic segmentation in complex outdoor park and off-road scenes characterized by irregular terrain, dense vegetation, and fine-grained ground categories.

To address this challenge, we propose LARS-3D, a novel Linear Global-Context Aggregation model for Robust Semantic Segmentation in 3D, integrating a linear global-context aggregation module within a sparse convolutional backbone. We also introduce EastPark, a novel large-scale multi-sensor dataset comprising over 9,000 annotated LiDAR scans with 22 terrain-aware semantic classes. We acquired and manually annotated it with a UGV to benchmark perception methods in outdoor, unstructured, complex public environments.

Our analysis reveals substantial cross-domain degradation between urban-trained models and terrain- and vegetation-rich scenes. Extensive experiments demonstrate consistent improvements over strong baselines on EastPark, RELLIS-3D, and SemanticPOSS, achieving competitive performance across unstructured outdoor and structured urban benchmarks. We provide a detailed ablation study showing that the global-context aggregation module improves the performance for outdoor 3D semantic segmentation. The implementation of LARS-3D and our EastPark dataset are published on this page.

Contributions

  • LARS-3D. A linear global-context aggregation model for robust 3D semantic segmentation, enabling efficient long-range contextual reasoning.
  • EastPark. A large-scale, multi-modal, terrain-rich dataset for unstructured outdoor environments: 9,056 manually annotated LiDAR scans across 22 classes, plus RGB-D images and IMU.
  • Evidence. Strong, competitive performance across unstructured and structured outdoor benchmarks, with analyses of efficiency and architectural design.
Method

Global context without quadratic attention

Sparse convolutions capture local geometry well but see only a limited neighbourhood; self-attention reasons globally but costs O(N²) in time and memory. LARS-3D keeps the sparse convolutional voxel branch of LSK3DNet and replaces its point branch with a global context aggregation (GCA) encoder that summarises the whole scan into a single normalised context vector, then gates it back into every point.

1

Downsample, then order

Points are merged by scaled voxel coordinates into Ns << N tokens. Because the token shift needs a sequence, a deterministic 3D Z-order (Morton) traversal linearises them so that spatial neighbours stay close in the stream.

2

Mix with the previous token

Each token is blended with its predecessor through learnable scalars μk, μv, μr, then projected into key, value and receptance — softplus on the key, a sigmoid gate on the receptance.

3

Aggregate once, gate everywhere

Instead of all-pairs attention, two sums over the whole scan give CKV and CK. Their ratio is a single global context vector, modulated per point by its own receptance gate and projected back with a residual.

Global context, in one pass
Ci = ∑j Kj ⊙ Vj ∑j Kj + ε ⊙ Ri

R

Two sums over the scan replace all-pairs attention. One GCA block costs O(NsC2); the encoder stacks L = 8 of them for O(LNsC2) — linear in the number of tokens Ns. Every point still sees the entire scan.

Dataset

EastPark: a natural park, labelled point by point

A UGV recorded four sequences in a public natural park on a clear day in mid-April 2025 — well-maintained trails, wilderness, and a range of ground materials. Two annotators labelled every scan: first globally, on the aggregated cloud registered by visual odometry, then refined per scan, with ambiguous cases reviewed jointly. Labels are limited to 30 m, beyond which returns are too sparse to annotate reliably.

Spatial ordering is what makes depth pay off

Each configuration was trained three times on the validation splits of all three datasets; the table reports mean mIoU with standard deviation. Random and Z-order GCA beat the baseline at every depth, and only the locality-preserving Z-order traversal keeps improving as the stack deepens — lexicographic ordering falls below the baseline at 8 blocks.

↑

Global context helps everywhere

Random and Z-order GCA beat the baseline at every depth — the gain comes from the aggregation itself, not from extra parameters. A gated linear feature mapping alone slightly hurts (−0.3 average).

⇄

Order decides whether depth helps

From 2 to 8 blocks, Z-order improves the average from 55.5 to 55.8, while lexicographic order collapses from 55.2 to 53.2 — below the 53.5 baseline. Preserving spatial locality in the 1D stream is what lets information propagate across blocks.

≈

The cost stays flat

Going from 2 to 8 blocks adds 4 ms and 29 MB. Linear complexity means depth is cheap, so the encoder can be stacked without the memory blow-up that quadratic attention would bring.
